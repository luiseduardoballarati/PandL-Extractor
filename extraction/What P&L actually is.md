# What a P&L Actually Is

Imagine you run a coffee shop.

## 1. Revenue

You sold **10,000 coffees at £3** each.

**Revenue = £30,000**

This is the money the business receives from selling its products or
services.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 2. Cost of Sales

You need coffee beans, milk, cups, and barista wages to make and sell
those coffees.

Suppose these direct costs total **£12,000**.

**Cost of Sales = £12,000**

These are the costs that are directly associated with producing what you
sold.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 3. Gross Profit

Now subtract the direct costs from revenue:

**Gross Profit = Revenue − Cost of Sales**

**£30,000 − £12,000 = £18,000**

Gross profit tells you what is left after paying the direct costs of the
products you sold.

A useful question here is:

> **Can the company sell its product for substantially more than it
> costs to produce it?**

This is closely related to **pricing power** and the economics of the
product itself.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 4. Operating Expenses

The coffee shop also has costs that are necessary to operate the
business but are not directly tied to each individual coffee.

Examples include:

-   Rent
-   Insurance
-   Accounting
-   Marketing

Suppose these operating expenses total **£10,000**.

**Operating Expenses = £10,000**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 5. Operating Income

Subtract operating expenses from gross profit:

**Operating Income = Gross Profit − Operating Expenses**

**£18,000 − £10,000 = £8,000**

Operating income shows how profitable the business is from its normal
operations, before considering financing and taxes.

A useful question here is:

> **Is the underlying business profitable once we account for the costs
> of actually running it?**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 6. Interest and Tax

The business has a loan, resulting in **£1,000 of interest expense**.

It also owes **£1,500 in tax**.

So:

**Interest = £1,000**

**Tax = £1,500**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 7. Net Income

Finally, subtract interest and tax from operating income:

**Net Income = Operating Income − Interest − Tax**

**£8,000 − £1,000 − £1,500 = £5,500**

**Net income = £5,500**

This is the **bottom line**: what remains after the business has
accounted for its operating costs, financing costs, and taxes.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# The Complete P&L

The whole structure can be summarized as:

``` text
Revenue                         £30,000
− Cost of Sales                 £12,000
──────────────────────────────────────
= Gross Profit                  £18,000

− Operating Expenses            £10,000
──────────────────────────────────────
= Operating Income               £8,000

− Interest                       £1,000
− Tax                            £1,500
──────────────────────────────────────
= Net Income                     £5,500
```

The key idea is that a **Profit & Loss statement (P&L)** starts with
revenue and subtracts different categories of costs in stages until it
reaches net income.

It is called a **statement** because it describes financial performance
**over a period of time**, such as a quarter or a year. It is not a
snapshot of the company's financial position at a particular moment.

# Why There Are Multiple Profit Lines

The different profit measures answer different questions.

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Measure                 What it tells you       Main question
  ----------------------- ----------------------- -----------------------
  **Gross Profit**        Profit after direct     Can the company charge
                          costs                   more than it costs to
                                                  produce what it sells?

  **Operating Income**    Profit after operating  Is the underlying
                          expenses                business profitable?

  **Net Income**          Profit after interest   How much profit remains
                          and tax                 after everything?
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

A company can have **strong gross profit but weak net income**.

For example, this could happen if the company has:

-   High operating expenses
-   Significant interest costs from debt
-   High tax expenses

That tells you something useful about *where* the company's
profitability is being lost. The economics of the product may be
attractive while the overall business remains less profitable because of
operating costs or financing.
